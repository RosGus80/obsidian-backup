K. Korsgaard - value in humanity:

TA(1)  
P1) As rational agents, we constantly have to make rational choices.  
P2) Whenever we make a rational choice, we cannot avoid implicitly evaluating the object of our choice, namely taking it to be good (Korsgaard 1996a, p. 115).  
P3) Whenever we evaluate any object of our choice, we implicitly evaluate ourselves, as rational agents, as the source of this value (e.g., Korsgaard 1996b, p. 167).  
C) As rational agents, we cannot but give value to ourselves as rational beings.

TA(2)  
(P′1) = (C) As rational agents, we cannot but give value to ourselves as rational beings.  
(P′2) If, as rational agents, we cannot but give value to ourselves as rational beings, we ought to consider ourselves valuable as rational beings.  
(P′3) If we ought to consider ourselves valuable as rational beings, we are valuable (as rational beings).  
(C′) As rational agents, we are valuable.

Gewrith's necessary dialectic method:

1. (1)  The author shows that every agent implicitly makes evaluative judgments about the goodness of his purposes and hence about the necessary goodness of freedom and well-being, which are necessary conditions of his acting, in order to reach any of his purposes. In this way, the gap between fact and value is overcome (Gewirth 1978, pp. 48ff.).
    
2. (2)  Becauseofthisnecessarygoodness,everyagentmakesadeonticjudgmentin which he claims that he has rights to freedom and well-being, namely that all other persons ought at least to refrain from interfering with the necessary conditions of his agency. A correlative ought claim on the part of the in- terlocutors corresponds, accordingly, to this right claim. In this way also the gap between is and ought can be overcome. At this point, however, the reason for the right claim is not yet moral, but prudential (Gewirth 1978, pp. 63ff.)
    
3. (3)  Since every agent must claim that he has these rights for the sufficient reason that he is a purposive agent, he must accept the generalization that all pur- posive agents have rights to freedom and well-being. A principle of universal- izability comes into play at this point, a principle that cannot be denied without self-contradiction for, in this case, the agent would be in the position of both affirming and denying that being a purposive agent is a sufficient condition of having rights of freedom and well-being (Gewirth 1978, p. 104). Thanks to this principle, the gap between prudential and moral reasons is filled, and it is finally possible to justify a supreme principle of morality, called “principle of generic consistency”, an egalitarian universalist moral principle that states the following:
    
    (PGC) “Act in accord with the generic rights of your recipients as well as of yourself.” (Gewirth 1978, pp. 129ff.)
